The RevOps AI Brief: Issue #5

Hi friends,

This week's pattern is hard to miss: practitioners stopped pitching dashboards and started shipping workers. Three builds below, all from people doing this in production right now, plus the governance shift that comes with it.

Let's get into it.

 

Build 1: A Slack-native deal caddy that does the win/loss analysis on every deal (not a sample)

Roe Han (founder of Octivian, ex-SMARP CEO) sat down on RevOps AF Episode 95 and described the exact failure mode most of us know: top 20% of reps drive 80% of revenue, you buy Gong + Clari + HubSpot + Salesforce + DR rooms, the gap doesn't close. His team spent hours a day reading emails and call recordings trying to find the why across 200 deals a quarter, then giving up and extrapolating from a small sample.

The build that actually moved the needle was structural:

  1. Run the AI deal-by-deal (never batch-summarize the whole pipeline at once, you get lazy extrapolation back).
  2. Have it identify the turning points per deal (multi-threading gap, no economic buyer, weak next-step cadence).
  3. Roll those up across segments to find the winning and losing patterns.
  4. Then (this is the unlock): don't tell the rep what to do, do the work for them. Draft the email to the economic buyer, propose the access path, hand it over for one click.
  5. Deliver it in Slack at the moment the deal needs it. Reps already have ten tools and don't want an eleventh.

Han's framing was the cleanest I've heard this year: the AI is a golf caddy, not a hammer. The rep still plays the game, the caddy carries the bag, picks the club, reads the green.

Tools: Octivian + Salesforce/HubSpot + Slack or Teams (Gong-style call data feeds the pattern analysis).

Impact: Han's claim, in his own words: the deal-by-deal approach gives you visibility his team never had even when three execs were manually reviewing calls. He flags an honest caveat too, that the breakthrough only landed once they stopped trying to be an 11th tool and started living where the rep already works.

Build it yourself:

  1. Pick one segment (one region, one product, one deal-size band). Don't boil the ocean.
  2. Pull the last 90 days of closed-won and closed-lost from your CRM with the linked call transcripts.
  3. Run an AI pass per deal (not in bulk) tagging: stakeholders touched, multi-threading depth, cadence gaps, competitive mentions, next-step quality.
  4. Roll up to find the 3-5 patterns that separate won from lost.
  5. Wire a Slack workflow that watches live deals for those same patterns and posts the recommended next action into the rep's DM (with a draft of the email or message, not just a nudge).

Build 2: A knowledge-graph 'brain' that orchestrates your agent stack (Lolita's Frankenstack fix)

Lolita Trachtengerts (VP GTM Ops & Growth at Spotlight AI) gave a great practitioner take on RevOps Unboxed this week. Two ideas worth lifting straight into your stack.

First, the copilot vs autopilot test she now uses with prospects: ChatGPT three months ago was a copilot (you do the work, it helps you draft). Claude with MCP today is autopilot (it takes the action). If your 'AI' tool still hands you insights and asks you to act, it's a copilot. Useful, but a different category.

Second (and this is the build): when you start running multiple agents across marketing, SDR, AE, and CS, you get what she calls a Frankenstack. The fix isn't more agents, it's an orchestrator. A knowledge graph (or methodology layer, or playbook store) that sits above the agents and guardrails what they're allowed to conclude and execute. Spotlight built theirs over four to five years of RevOps domain knowledge. You don't have that runway, but you can start small.

Tools: Spotlight AI (their own MCP server connected to Claude is the pattern she demoed), or roll your own using Claude with custom skills + your CRM playbooks + win/loss patterns.

Impact: Lolita's stated outcome on the GTM side: 'we don't do spray and pray', they keep outreach volume tight and use agents to monitor multiple platforms for intent signals, and she reports 'higher conversion rates' from the more personalized sequences. She doesn't put a number on it, fair to treat as directional.

Build it yourself:

  1. Write down (in a single markdown file or doc) the 5-10 plays your team actually runs: 'when a champion changes jobs, do X', 'when product usage drops 30%, do Y'.
  2. Add your ICP definition, your disqualification rules, and your top 3 winning patterns from Build 1.
  3. Stand up a Claude project (or a single agent) that has only that doc as context. Every other agent (enrichment, scoring, drafting) calls into it for guidance.
  4. When an agent wants to take an action, it has to cite which play it's executing. If it can't, the action is blocked.
  5. Review the action log weekly. Update the doc. That doc is your brain.

Build 3: Run a 30-day autonomous coverage pod (so you find out where the guardrails actually need to be)

Tim Cortinovis ran a sharp Agented Revenue Brief episode on the bigger shift this week: Salesforce calling agents the top growth tactic, Stripe opening its Link wallet to agents, Google Gemini letting AI complete purchases end to end, Meta monetizing agent sales inside WhatsApp and Instagram. His takeaway, which he keeps hammering: governance, not capability, is now the bottleneck.

His concrete prescription for CROs and RevOps leaders is the cleanest experiment design I've seen this month: a 30-day autonomous coverage pod.

Tools: any agent platform you already trust (Salesforce Agentforce, n8n + Claude, Spotlight, custom) + Slack + your CRM with strict permission scopes.

Impact: Cortinovis's framing, paraphrased: the goal of the pod isn't 'AI efficiency', it's proving you can run autonomy with trust intact. The companies winning aren't the ones with the most AI features, they're the ones with the clearest accountability model for what machines are allowed to do.

Build it yourself:

  1. Pick a tightly-bounded slice: SMB renewals, or inbound mid-market under $25k ACV, or one product line.
  2. Let one agent own every touch in that slice for 30 days: triage, follow-up, scheduling, quoting.
  3. Set hard escalation triggers up front (deal > $X, contract redline, legal flag, competitor mention).
  4. Log every agent action (who, what, when, why) to a single audit table that RevOps owns.
  5. At day 30, review three numbers: agent completion rate (not adoption), human escalation rate, and trust incidents. That tells you where to widen or tighten the guardrails for the next slice.

Where this is heading: RevOps stops reporting on revenue and starts running autonomy

Two threads converged this week and it's worth naming.

One, Lolita's closing line, which she warned was controversial: your pipeline is a lie. Most enterprise pipelines don't survive an honest review. Reps inflate, CRM hygiene rots, leaders nod through the forecast call anyway. That's the broken foundation any agent sits on top of.

Two, Cortinovis's reframing of the RevOps job description: from reporting on revenue to running autonomy operations. Setting permission levels, authoring playbooks, enforcing escalation, maintaining real-time auditability. If RevOps isn't at the table when agents get deployed, you get agent-led chaos and every team builds its own bot.

The practical implication for the next quarter: before you ship Build 3, do the unglamorous thing first. Pick one segment, fix the CRM data underneath it, write down the plays the team actually runs, and then let an agent loose on it. Same lesson we keep landing on: agents amplify whatever the data foundation says, for better or worse. Get the foundation right on a small slice, then expand.
