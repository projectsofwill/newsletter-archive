The RevOps AI Brief: Issue #6

Good morning. This week pulled in three different directions and they actually rhyme.

The first thread is architectural: a wave of practitioners is arguing that the next RevOps build isn't another workflow, it's a control layer (a data catalog plus a set of custom tools) that sits between your AI agents and your CRM. The second is that the big sales-execution vendors are all shipping forecast explainability in the same month, which is a tell about what buyers actually want from agents this year. And the third is the quiet one: when teams pick a CRM in 2026, the deciding factor often isn't AI features at all, it's whether LeanData runs on the platform.

Three builds and a close, as always.

 

Build 1: The control layer that lets agents touch your CRM (without IT shutting it down)

The setup. Ed King, CEO of Openprise, made a sharp claim on the RevOpsAF podcast this week: roughly 95% of enterprise AI projects are stuck in pilot because agents are not allowed near core systems like Salesforce or HubSpot. Only about 5% of agents actually update a CRM record, and almost none in enterprise. His read on why: hallucination has not improved in five years, IT and InfoSec are right to say no, and bolting middleware on top is a slippery slope.

His fix is the architectural pattern worth stealing this week. Instead of pointing agents at raw CRM APIs, RevOps builds a control layer with two parts:

  1. A data catalog: 20 to 30 curated datasets (`customers_last_12_months`, `abm_accounts`, `whitespace`) that hide your custom objects and bastardized schema from the model.
  2. A tool catalog: deterministic MCP servers and APIs (`create_campaign`, `update_status`, `route_lead`) that enforce naming conventions, funnel transitions, and governance on the way in.

The agent calls tools. The tools call the system. No token gets burned re-deriving who your customers are, and no agent ever does a free-form write to Salesforce.

Tools: Openprise (or any orchestration platform you can run no-code), custom MCP servers, your CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot, Dynamics), Claude / Agentforce / the agent UI of your choice.

Impact (King's own framing): in his customer base, the teams that exposed raw schemas to agents could not reliably answer 'who are my customers' even once. Teams that pre-built a catalog cut tokens, latency, and error rate, and unblocked IT approval. Take these as the speaker's own claims, not benchmarks.

Build it yourself:

  1. Pick one painful, repeated question your team asks the CRM (start with `customers_last_12_months` or `open_pipeline_by_segment`). Materialize it as one clean table.
  2. Wrap two write actions you trust as MCP tools (`update_opportunity_stage`, `log_call`). Bake your governance (required fields, naming) inside the tool.
  3. Give the agent only the catalog and the tools, never the raw API. Log every call.
  4. Add one dataset and one tool per sprint. Treat the catalog like a product backlog, owned by RevOps, not IT.
  5. Measure: % of agent actions that route through the layer, time to add a new capability, tokens per query.

Build 2: Forecast explainability is suddenly a category (Einstein, Gong, and Outreach all shipped it in the same window)

The setup. Three sales-execution vendors all pushed the same idea in the last few weeks: stop showing the AI's forecast number, start showing why the AI disagrees with the rep. Treat this as a category shift, not three coincidences.

  • Salesforce Einstein Copilot for Pipeline Inspection expanded GA. Reps and managers can ask 'what changed in my Q3 pipeline this week?' and get an AI summary of deal movements, risk reasons, and upside, with the forecast linked back to coverage, stage conversion, and historical behavior.
  • Gong added an 'AI vs human forecast' delta view and a per-deal risk explanation card (e.g. 'no economic buyer present, last conversation 27 days ago, no mutual plan'). These export straight into QBR decks.
  • Outreach Kaia Forecast now pushes call-detected risks (competitor named, scope creep, pricing objection) as fields into the CRM, so they flow into scoring and forecasting downstream.

The shared assumption: a forecast number with no reason behind it does not survive a CRO meeting. The win for RevOps is that the features now exist; the job becomes making sure the inputs (clean stages, logged activity, identified buying committee) are actually there.

Tools: Salesforce Einstein Copilot / Sales Cloud, Gong, Outreach Kaia, your CRM activity capture pipeline.

Impact (vendor framing): Salesforce highlights faster forecast review and risk callouts at the rep level. Gong emphasizes auditability in QBRs. Outreach focuses on bringing conversational risk signals into structured CRM fields. Treat the specifics in vendor enablement as directional.

Build it yourself:

  1. Pick one explainability surface to standardize on this quarter. Don't run three.
  2. Audit your deal-risk inputs first: economic buyer field, last activity, mutual action plan, competitor field. If half are empty, the AI explanations will read as noise.
  3. Add a required 'risk reason' picklist to opportunities. Map vendor signals into it (so Kaia's competitor flag and a rep's manual flag land in the same field).
  4. In the next forecast call, replace 'what's the number' with 'where do you disagree with the AI'. Track which reps' overrides actually beat the model over the next two quarters.
  5. Roll the same risk card into renewals (CS Ops). The model and the inputs are the same.

Build 3: AI-native outbound is a different shape than 'AI inside your sequencer'

The setup. St John Dalgleish, CEO of Pearlon AI, made the cleanest version of an argument worth taking seriously: 'your outbound looks like everybody else's, and your buyers can tell'. His read: the last real innovation in outbound was the email sequence (over a decade ago), and bolting AI personalization on top of the same sequencer does not solve the actual problem. Buyers pattern-match the template, not the variables.

The RevOps angle is not 'buy this tool'. It's that the outbound stack a lot of teams own (Salesloft or Outreach plus a personalization layer) is the wrong unit of analysis if every competitor runs the same one. He also called out the failure mode that matters for ops: senior sales leaders buy the AI tooling, hand it to the team, and stop paying attention to the reports. The deployments that work are the ones where the VP of Sales is closest to the outputs.

Tools: Pearlon AI (AI-native outbound), incumbent sequencers (Salesloft, Outreach), Clay for the enrichment / scoring layer, your CRM as the source of truth.

Impact (Dalgleish's own framing): he claims the teams getting real lift are the ones running AI-native outbound as a distinct channel, with their VP of Sales involved weekly in reviewing what the system actually sent. No public conversion numbers were shared.

Build it yourself:

  1. Pull a sample of 50 outbound emails your team sent last week. Strip names and company. If you cannot tell which rep wrote which, your output is template-shaped.
  2. Carve out one segment (one persona or one ICP slice) as your AI-native test. Don't let it inherit your existing sequence library.
  3. Define one weekly review where the senior sales leader reads 10 raw outputs. No dashboards.
  4. Wire the outbound system to write what it sent and why back to the CRM as a structured field. This is what makes attribution and forecasting work later (and connects to Build 1).
  5. Kill any sequence whose reply rate is below your baseline at the four-week mark. Treat outbound experiments like a product backlog with a hit rate, not a permanent stack.

Where this is heading: the boring integration still picks your CRM in 2026

GrowthSpree pulled together a useful comparison this week across 300+ B2B companies on Salesforce vs HubSpot for 2026. The AI features got the headlines (HubSpot's Breeze hits time-to-value in about 2 weeks, Agentforce takes 6 to 10 plus prompt engineering and about $2 per autonomous conversation), but their own conclusion is that the decisive integration is not an AI feature. It's LeanData.

LeanData runs on Salesforce only. If your routing, lead-to-account matching, or multi-thread buying committee orchestration depend on it (and at $50M+ ARR they usually do), no amount of native AI on the other platform closes the gap. Their benchmark: roughly 70% of ABM programs under $25M run on HubSpot, roughly 85% over $50M run on Salesforce.

The takeaway for RevOps planning right now: when you're evaluating an AI-heavy CRM motion, run the LeanData and CPQ check first. Those constraints decide which agents you can even build on top. Everything else (Breeze vs Agentforce, attribution, copilot UX) is downstream of that one call.
